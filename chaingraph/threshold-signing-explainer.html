Threshold signing: one key across many holders

One signing key can sit in several hands at once, so that two of three holders together can sign and any single holder alone cannot. This page walks one 2-of-3 ceremony end to end twice: once with a trusted dealer who briefly holds the whole key, and once with distributed key generation, where the whole key is never assembled anywhere. It closes with the failure stories bad randomness keeps writing, and with where threshold ideas touch this estate. Every ceremony value on this page is a recorded transcript: it was generated offline by a pinned cryptography library before this page was written, and the page itself runs no cryptography.

Part one · The problem

One key is one point of failure

Two steps: what a verifier actually sees when a signature arrives, and how one key can be split so that no single holder alone can use it.

1
Step 1 · The problem

From one key to many holders

One ordinary signing key answers three questions at once: who approves, where the key lives, and what happens when that one place burns. Threshold signing changes the middle answer: the same single key, in effect, lives in several hands, and a quorum of those hands must agree before anything is signed.

A multisignature scheme keeps separate keys and produces separate signatures, so the policy is visible on the wire: a verifier counts three signatures and learns that three parties were involved. A threshold scheme produces one signature under one public key, and the quorum policy stays private to the holders. That privacy is a design choice with a cost: the ceremony that splits the key has to be run well, which is the subject of the next three steps.

Step 2 · The problem

Shares and thresholds

A threshold scheme cuts one secret into n shares so that any t of them can act together, while fewer than t learn nothing about the secret. This page's ceremony uses t = 2 and n = 3: two holders can sign, one holder alone cannot, and all three together are never needed.

The mathematics is polynomial interpolation over a finite field: fix a secret as the constant term of a random polynomial of degree t-1, hand each holder one point on that polynomial, and any t points determine the polynomial while t-1 points leave it completely undetermined. The commitments are the polynomial's coefficient points, published so shares can be checked. FROST, the signing protocol used on this page, builds its 2-of-3 group exactly this way.

Two ways to cut one key into three shares

Step 3 · The ceremony

The trusted dealer trackdesign scene

The oldest way to cut a key: a dealer generates the whole key, cuts it into shares, hands one share to each holder, and deletes the key. It works, and it has a structural weakness: for a moment, one machine held everything, and the ceremony is only as trustworthy as that machine and its randomness.

The recorded dealer run drew the whole key first, then cut it into the three shares shown, each share paired with a public verifying share so holder 1 can check what it received. RFC 9591, the FROST specification, sketches exactly this shape in its Appendix C, because key generation is out of that document's scope. The whole-key value above is real and is published deliberately: this ceremony was recorded for teaching, the key was born in a sealed offline run, and showing what the dealer saw is the point. That exposure is why the next step exists.

Step 4 · The ceremony

Distributed key generation

Distributed key generation removes the dealer: every holder contributes randomness, the shares are formed in public round messages, and the whole signing key exists only as a sum that is never assembled anywhere. The ceremony holds together as long as one participant is honest.

This three-round shape, with the proof of knowledge in round one, is the distributed key generation implemented in @noble/curves 2.4.0, the Zcash Foundation construction. RFC 9591 covers the FROST signing that follows; it deliberately leaves key generation out of scope, so the DKG wording here names the library's construction rather than the RFC. In the recorded run, all three holders derived byte-identical group public keys and mutually consistent verifying shares, and each share validated against the published commitments.

Step 5 · The ceremony

Two holders sign as one

Signing is RFC 9591 FROST signing, in two rounds: the participating holders publish nonce commitments, then each produces a partial signature, and an aggregation step folds the partials into one ordinary 64-byte Schnorr signature under the group public key.

The message signed in both recorded runs was the ASCII string threshold-signing-explainer recorded ceremony. The recorded transcript also includes, for completeness, the dealer run signing by holders 1 and 2. Every verification below passed in the recorded run, and the second one is the quietly important result: the aggregate is a well-formed RFC 8032 Ed25519 signature, so an ordinary verifier with no FROST code accepts it.

Part three · Choices, failures, and this estate

From holder choice to failure stories

Four steps: choosing holders as a design value, FROST against MuSig2, the randomness failure stories with their primary sources, and where threshold ideas touch the AINumbers estate.

6
Step 6 · Choices

Choosing who holds a share

A threshold is only as strong as the spread of its holders. Holder choice is a design value: pick holders by jurisdiction, by authentication type, and by whether each is a person or an organisation, so that one compromise, one legal demand, or one mistake stays below the quorum.

The three axes do different work. Jurisdiction spread keeps one court or one regulator from reaching a quorum. Authentication spread means one class of credential compromise, for example a stolen passkey, reaches at most one share. The person-or-organisation choice sets who is accountable and who is reachable in an emergency. A sound policy names its holders along all three axes before the ceremony is scheduled.

Step 7 · Choices

FROST and MuSig2 side by side

Two Schnorr multi-party schemes answer different questions. FROST, specified in RFC 9591 (an Informational RFC), lets a threshold subset of n sign. MuSig2, specified in BIP 327 (a draft Bitcoin Improvement Proposal), requires all participants and exists to make several keys look like one ordinary BIP340 key.

For custody of one key across several holders, the threshold column is the one that matters: MuSig2 cannot express 2-of-3, because all participants must sign. MuSig2 shines where a fixed group wants the efficiency and privacy of one key. Both live or die by nonce discipline, which is the next step's subject.

Step 8 · Failures

Why bad randomness is the recurring failure

Threshold structures spread a key across holders, and every one of those holders still draws randomness: to generate shares, and for every single nonce at signing. The recorded failure stories below are all, at bottom, randomness stories, and each is linked to its primary source.

The Debian OpenSSL weakness

Between 17 September 2006 and 13 May 2008, a Debian-specific change to the OpenSSL package made its random number generator predictable, so cryptographic key material was guessable. The Debian advisory DSA 1571-1 (CVE-2008-0166) told every affected user to recreate keys: SSH keys, OpenVPN keys, DNSSEC keys, and key material for X.509 certificates and TLS sessions, and it flagged DSA signing keys as compromised outright, because DSA signs with a fresh secret random value each time. Two years of keys, one quiet packaging change.

Debian DSA 1571-1 (primary source)CVE-2008-0166

Serial-number entropy misses at certificate authorities

Three public compliance cases in 2019, none of them key generation: certificate serial numbers. The Baseline Requirements ask certificate authorities for at least 64 bits of output from a cryptographically secure random number generator, and three authorities landed just under it. GoDaddy's serials carried about 63.994 bits after a 2016 change, Entrust's AffirmTrust issuers produced 63-bit serials through an EJBCA flaw, and DarkMatter's platform was configured for 64-bit serials that left about 63 bits of entropy. All three were disclosed on the Mozilla bug tracker, remediated, and the affected certificates re-issued or revoked.

Bugzilla 1533774, GoDaddy serial entropyBugzilla 1536287, AffirmTrust EJBCA serialsBugzilla 1531800, DarkMatter serial entropy

The lesson for a threshold ceremony: the shares and the nonces both come from randomness, so the ceremony's health is the health of its entropy sources at every draw. The estate's Ed25519 guide covers where per-signature randomness sits in the signatures used here.

Step 9 · The estate

In the AINumbers estate

Two statements close this page: what a threshold signer would change for this estate's verifiers, which is design only, and the k-of-n structure the estate runs today, which is a set of independent cosignatures rather than one shared key.

How it could plug indesign

A FROST-Ed25519 threshold signer would produce one ordinary Ed25519 signature under one group public key. The estate's receipt verifier T568 and envelope verifier T624 already verify ordinary Ed25519 signatures, so both would accept such a signature with no code change on their side. The threshold signer itself is a design on this page: the estate runs no threshold signing today, and this page runs no cryptography at all.

The estate does this todaylive

The estate's real k-of-n today is the Sigsum witness set behind anchor.ainumbers.co: twelve pinned witnesses, each an independent key on its own device, cosigning every transparency-log checkpoint, with verification requiring a quorum of those independent cosignatures. That is k-of-n agreement among separate signatures, shown here as exactly that, rather than one threshold key. Measured 23 October 2026 from anchor-suite/public/lib/sigsum.mjs (witness list last changed 20 August 2026).

Anchors and transparency logsT625, one verifier for three logsThe evidence estateSignatures and key retirement

The distinction matters for anyone tempted to read a witness quorum as threshold signing. Each Sigsum witness signs with its own key, visibly, and the policy counts the cosignatures; a FROST quorum produces one signature and keeps the policy private. Both are k-of-n trust, and they are different mechanisms.
