How OpenChainGraph works

A decision tool produces artifacts. Each artifact carries a fingerprint of its inputs and outputs, and each one can cite the fingerprints of the artifacts it consumed. The result is a verifiable provenance chain that any agent or auditor can re-check with nothing more than a hash function. This page walks the standard from that first idea to time anchoring. The version of record is v0.8.13, and the matching normative text lives in the technical specification.

Presenter mode shows one step per screen. Arrow keys move, A toggles autoplay, Esc exits.
Every claim on this page reduces to one move: take the recorded inputs and outputs, run SHA-256 over them, and compare the result with the fingerprint the artifact carries. When the numbers match, the record is intact.
Part 1

The idea everything rests on

Four short steps before any standard vocabulary. A hash, a school project, a cheat attempt and a loan application carry the whole picture.

1
Foundation · Beginner

What a hash is

OpenChainGraph is built on a concept called cryptographic hashing. A hash is a digital fingerprint for any piece of data: a document, a number, a decision. Feed any data into a hash function and it produces a fixed string. The standard uses SHA-256, and the fingerprint is always 64 characters.

SPEC §4Live Hash Verifier

One changed letter rewrites all 64 characters of the fingerprint

Try it yourself, live SHA-256

Type anything below. The 64-character fingerprint recomputes with every keystroke. Change a single character, even one space, and the entire output transforms.

SHA-256 output
Characters changed vs. original

The same input gives the same output every time

Run SHA-256 on "Apple" a million times in a million different countries and you always get the same hash. This property is called determinism.

A tiny change rewrites the whole fingerprint

One character difference flips the entire 64-character output. Any tampering, even changing a single digit in a decision output, is immediately detectable.

The fingerprint cannot be reversed

Given only the hash you cannot work backward to the original data. A fingerprint is not encryption. The only verification is to re-run the hash yourself.

Why the output looks random

The avalanche effect is intentional. Two inputs that differ by one character produce hashes with zero characters in common, which is what makes tampering impossible to miss.

Plain language · Beginner

Imagine you're submitting a group project

Before the math and the JSON, here is the same idea with something you already know.

You write a report and get it stamped

You finish your history essay. Before handing it in, the teacher runs your paper through a machine that produces a unique 64-character code: your paper's fingerprint. Change even one word afterwards and the machine produces a completely different code. In OpenChainGraph a decision tool runs a calculation and SHA-256 hashes its inputs and outputs. That hash is the fingerprint, the execution_hash.

execution_hash

Your teammate cites your work and includes your stamp

Your teammate writes the next section. At the top of her section she writes: "This builds on Roger's section, fingerprint: a3f9…". Her work is now linked to yours by your fingerprint. If anyone later swaps in a different version of your section, her reference no longer matches and the tamper is caught at once. In OpenChainGraph, Tool B lists Tool A's execution_hash in its chain.parent_hashes array. The link is the hash itself; a file name or a URL would not detect the swap.

chain.parent_hashes

The teacher verifies the whole chain without trusting anyone

The teacher takes your section, re-runs it through the fingerprint machine, and checks whether the code matches what your teammate wrote. She then does the same for your teammate's section. She never calls you and never emails you. The math is the proof. In OpenChainGraph, any agent or auditor re-hashes {policy_parameters, output_payload} and compares the result with the stored execution_hash. A match means verified, and no trusted authority is required.

verify_execution_hash

A student from another school joins under the same rules

A student from a different school cites your group's project in her own work. She knows neither your teacher nor your systems, yet she can verify the fingerprints herself because the fingerprint standard is public and open. Her citation creates a three-link chain across two schools with no phone calls asking anyone to confirm versions. In OpenChainGraph this is cross-vendor chaining: two organizations that have never met form a verifiable provenance chain by following the same open specification.

x-chaingraph capability
Replace the history essay with a credit risk score, the teacher with a bank regulator, and the student from another school with a fintech vendor. The protocol is identical, and that is the point. OpenChainGraph is the fingerprint standard applied to financial decisions.
3
Plain language · Beginner

What happens when someone tries to cheat?

The whole point of the fingerprint system is that nothing can be changed in secret. When a bad actor tweaks one number inside an artifact, even a tiny edit, the chain reports it.

A tampered artifact breaks every citation downstream of it

After tampering

Changing even one byte of an artifact produces a completely different SHA-256 hash. Every downstream artifact that cited the old hash now holds a broken reference. The mismatch is instant and obvious, with no investigation needed.

Why this protects you

There is no central authority to bribe or fool. The math runs locally on any verifier's machine. A bad actor cannot quietly edit a credit score, a risk rating or a loan decision: any change breaks every downstream link in the chain.

Stage 1 of 22 · Beginner · v0.1

A decision tool runs and emits an artifact

Every conformant tool takes structured inputs (policy_parameters), runs a deterministic computation, and produces outputs (output_payload). It wraps inputs and outputs together into a JSON envelope and runs SHA-256 over the combined package. That hash, the execution_hash, is stamped onto the artifact before it is sent anywhere.

SPEC §1Live Hash VerifierTechnical spec

The document leaving the tool is labeled "raw result" and has no hash yet. It becomes a "JSON payload" inside the artifact envelope, and only then does SHA-256 compute the fingerprint.

Deterministic compute

Same inputs always produce identical outputs. No timestamps and no random numbers inside the hash preimage: those would make the hash different every second and break reproducibility.

The artifact envelope

A JSON object wrapping the inputs (policy_parameters) and outputs (output_payload) together. Adding the execution_hash turns it into a verifiable record.

The execution hash

SHA-256 computed over {"policy_parameters":…, "output_payload":…} with keys sorted alphabetically. Any party can re-run this exact procedure to verify the hash independently.

The mandate type

For example risk_control, liquidity_mandate or credit_assessment. A category label that tells downstream tools and agents what kind of decision this artifact represents.

Stage 2 of 22 · Beginner · v0.1

Chains form when artifacts cite each other

When Tool B consumes the output of Tool A, it copies Tool A's execution_hash into its own chain.parent_hashes array. That single act creates a cryptographically linked chain. Tool C can cite both A and B. The result is a directed acyclic graph, a family tree of decisions in which every connection is a hash reference that can be verified independently.

A named chain of steps like this is a workflow in reader-facing terms. Package a workflow with a signed manifest, declared inputs, sample data and review gates, and it becomes a pack ready to run in Helm.

SPEC §1SPEC §0.1Live SandboxChain Builder

What depth means, from a starting point to three links back

Depth 0. The starting point. This artifact has no parents and was built from raw inputs only. In the loan scene above the credit score tool is depth 0.

Depth 1. One link back. This artifact consumed a depth-0 artifact. The affordability tool is depth 1.

Depth 2. Two links back. Its parents are themselves derived from original sources. The risk model cites the credit and affordability artifacts, so it sits at depth 2.

Depth 3. Three links back. The loan approval cites the risk model, so verifying it fully means tracing three steps to the root. Depth is the length of the longest path from any root, so branches are never double-counted.

Tool, node and chain are three different scopes

Tool: the callable unit itself, either a browser page like the work-mandate compiler or the identical computation exposed as an MCP-callable tool that an agent calls directly. SPEC §21 references it as tool_id. Same computation, two doors in.

Node: a tool registered in the Graph Index, viewed as one vertex of the citation graph. It exists once registered, whether or not any chain's step list places it; a chain's steps[] entry is one way a node picks up citation edges rather than what makes it a node (SPEC §0.1). The network-topology tools under Guides use "node" for a DLT network participant, an unrelated meaning.

Chain: the named, ordered list of steps itself, a chaingraph.json chains[] entry. The Escalation SLA / Supervised-Autonomy Receipt chain, for example, places the work-mandate compiler (art-274-compile-work-mandate) as its first node, feeding the agentic-AI risk classifier as its second.

Why this matters

An auditor can pick any node in the chain, re-run the tool with the recorded policy_parameters, recompute the SHA-256 hash, and compare. A hash mismatch means the artifact was altered or the tool is non-deterministic. No signer and no certificate authority are involved; trust comes from reproducibility, and you can verify it yourself in your own browser.

Stage 3 of 22 · Practitioner · v0.1

An agent calls and verifies over MCP

A conformant tool exposes a tools/call MCP endpoint. An AI assistant such as Claude or ChatGPT, or any MCP-compatible agent, can call emit_artifact, receive the envelope, then call verify_execution_hash to confirm integrity. The agent never needs to trust the server: it runs the SHA-256 check itself, the same way the demo above ran "Apple" through SHA-256. When the numbers match, the artifact is genuine.

SPEC §7Live Hash VerifierLive Sandbox

One MCP round-trip out and an independent re-hash back

The agent reads before it calls

The agent sends policy_parameters and receives the full artifact envelope. It modifies nothing: the server declares readOnlyHint: true, so nothing on the server changes from the call.

verify_execution_hash

Given an artifact, the server re-runs the same SHA-256 procedure and returns match or mismatch. Agents call this on every artifact before trusting it, the way you would double-check a bank balance.

build_chaingraph

Given a goal or chain name, returns the ordered list of tools to call with the parent_hashes wiring done. It is the agent's recipe: the exact order of steps, nothing left to guess.

The Graph Index catalog

A published JSON document listing all tools, their mandate_type, and which tools consume which outputs. Agents read it first to plan a chain before calling anything, the way you read a menu before ordering.

Platform integration · Practitioner

AIN Bridge wires inputs inside the browser

OCG defines the artifact format and the hash. AIN Bridge is how AINumbers tools actually receive inputs from agents, composers and prior tool outputs, entirely in the browser, with no server round-trips and no storage writes. Every tool in the suite carries a small Bridge script that listens on three channels and fills form inputs the moment the page loads.

The OpenChainGraph standard covers provenance structure, hashing and cross-vendor chaining. AIN Bridge is the AINumbers implementation of how that chaining reaches a browser tool. The standard allows any transport that populates tool inputs; Bridge is one such transport, designed to be zero-network and zero-egress.

Prefill deep-link

Encode {"elementId": value, …} as base64url and append it as a URL hash fragment: tool.html#in=<token>. Add &run=1 to auto-execute. The fragment is never sent to a server; it lives only in the browser. Agents hand off a pre-configured tool URL to a user with one click.

Composer messaging

Composer pages load tools in <iframe> elements and drive them via window.postMessage. Three message types: AIN_PREFILL fills inputs, AIN_RUN triggers execution, AIN_COLLECT retrieves the output Policy Mandate. Same-origin only; cross-origin composers cannot drive tools.

Policy Mandate intake

Tools with intake: true render a drop zone. Drag, choose or paste a .policy.json from any prior tool. Bridge validates mandate_id, tool_id and payload, then maps payload keys to matching element IDs, the same mechanism as prefill driven by mandate instead of URL. The execution_hash field in the mandate closes the provenance loop.

The channels never leave the browser

All three channels decode client-side. URL hash fragments are not included in HTTP requests. postMessage is same-origin only. Policy Mandate JSON never leaves the browser tab. Bridge dispatches input and change events after filling so reactive tool UIs update correctly.

Stage 5 of 22 · Advanced · v0.2

The semantic context maps artifacts to W3C PROV-DM

v0.2 adds a @context URL to every artifact. That one field makes OpenChainGraph artifacts interpretable by any W3C-compliant provenance tool. Field names like tool_id map to standardized PROV-DM terms, so a DORA audit system, a prov:Entity processor and a DCAT catalog can all consume the same artifact without a custom adapter. A buildType URI declares exactly which hash construction was used, making the provenance machine-auditable end to end.

W3C PROV-DM GuidebuildType / SLSA Guide

JSON-LD semantic context

A single URL (https://ainumbers.co/chaingraph/context/v0.3/context.jsonld) maps all OpenChainGraph field names to W3C standard vocabulary. Any JSON-LD processor understands the artifact automatically.

Parent hash mapping

parent_hashes maps to prov:wasDerivedFrom, the standard W3C term for an entity derived from another entity. Auditors using any PROV-aware tool see the chain automatically.

Hash algorithm declaration

https://ainumbers.co/chaingraph/context/v0.2#WebCryptoSHA256 declares the exact construction: sorted-key JSON, then UTF-8, then WebCrypto SHA-256. No ambiguity about what was hashed or how.

Machine-discoverable graph index

chaingraph.json is expressible as a dcat:Catalog with each tool as a dcat:Dataset. Any data catalog portal can index an OpenChainGraph suite without a custom connector.

Stage 6 of 22 · Advanced · v0.2

In-toto envelopes and Ed25519 signing add authenticity

The execution_hash proves integrity: the content has not changed. Authenticity is a separate question: who produced it. v0.2 upgrades audit_signature from a bare string to a structured in-toto ITE-6 attestation envelope, {payloadType, payload, signatures[{keyid, sig}]}. An optional Ed25519 key (RFC 8032, via WebCrypto) signs over the execution_hash. These are two independent checks: a valid hash with an invalid signature means the content is intact but the signer is wrong.

in-toto GuideEd25519 Guide

The attestation envelope

The upgrade turns audit_signature into a structured object: payloadType (MIME type), payload (base64url), and a signatures array with keyid and sig per signer.

A signature over the hash

An optional WebCrypto Ed25519 key pair signs over the execution_hash bytes. Small (32-byte keys, 64-byte signatures), fast, and natively available in modern browsers via crypto.subtle.

Integrity and authenticity are independent

Integrity means the content is unchanged (hash check). Authenticity means it came from the right key (signature check). Both can pass, both can fail, or either can fail alone. Always verify both separately.

The keyid field

The keyid in each signature entry identifies which public key was used. Verifiers look up the public key by keyid to confirm the signature, which enables key rotation without re-signing old artifacts.

Stage 7 of 22 · Advanced · v0.2

Chains appear as distributed traces in OTel

v0.2 defines a deterministic mapping from OpenChainGraph artifacts to OpenTelemetry spans. The first 8 bytes of an artifact's execution_hash become its spanId. The first 8 bytes of parent_hashes[0] become the parentSpanId. The root artifact's execution_hash becomes the traceId for the whole chain. Run artifactToOtelSpan() on any artifact and it drops into Datadog, Jaeger or Honeycomb as a waterfall trace, with no instrumentation code required.

Artifact identity in OTel

The first 8 bytes (16 hex characters) of execution_hash become the span ID. The mapping is deterministic: the same artifact always maps to the same spanId, with no random IDs and no coordination.

The chain link in OTel

The first 8 bytes of parent_hashes[0] become the parentSpanId, pointing at the parent artifact's span. This is what creates the waterfall indentation in Datadog, Jaeger and Honeycomb.

One identifier for the chain

The root artifact's execution_hash (depth 0, no parents) becomes the traceId for the entire chain. Every artifact in the chain shares it, grouping the run into one coherent trace.

Zero-instrumentation tracing

artifactToOtelSpan() is a pure function: it takes an artifact and returns an OTel span object. No SDK, no agents, no code changes to existing tools. Export the span to any OTel-compatible backend and decision chains become observable.

Stage 8 of 22 · Advanced · v0.3

The ISO 20022 semantic profile aligns the payload

v0.3 gives the financial payload a semantic standard to complement the provenance envelope's W3C PROV-DM alignment. The ISO 20022 semantic profile is an optional JSON-LD overlay, a second @context entry, that maps amount, currency, party and agent fields to ISO 20022 business elements. It never touches the execution_hash preimage (§2 is unchanged). Tools that are not payment tools carry no profile: a VaR engine or DORA classifier has nothing ISO 20022-shaped to declare.

SPEC §8ISO 20022 Guide

What alignment means here

OpenChainGraph aligns with ISO 20022 vocabulary without claiming to be a registered ISO 20022 message. The profile maps common financial fields to ISO 20022 business element names so banks, PSPs and regulators can read artifacts without a custom adapter, using vocabulary they already know.

Two context URLs in an array

Applying the profile means adding a second entry to the @context array: the existing OCG context plus iso20022-context.jsonld. No other field changes, and the preimage is unaffected, so a v0.1 verifier produces an identical hash.

External code sets

Currency (ISO 4217), country (ISO 3166), BIC/SWIFT (ISO 9362), and LEI (ISO 17442) code sets are adopted by reference, the same external code lists ISO 20022 payments already use. No new registries; no proprietary enumerations.

The fields with an ISO 20022 counterpart

Only fields with a direct ISO 20022 counterpart are mapped: amount, currency, debtor, creditor, lei, bicfi, settlement_date, remittance_information. Risk scores, model outputs and compliance flags are not profiled.

Stage 9 of 22 · Advanced · v0.3

LEI anchors and self-describing key identifiers name the parties

v0.3 formalizes two optional identity anchors. A did:key identifier, a self-describing multicodec-prefixed base58btc encoding of the Ed25519 public key, is the recommended form for keyid in the signature envelope. An ISO 17442 Legal Entity Identifier (LEI) optionally binds the publisher, and the signing key, to a regulator-grade legal-entity anchor. Together they bridge the signing layer (§6) and the ISO 20022 payload layer (§8): the same LEI can identify both the tool vendor and a debtor or creditor in a payment artifact.

SPEC §9Ed25519 / did:key Guide

Self-describing key identity

A did:key:z6Mk… URI encodes the raw Ed25519 public key in multicodec plus base58btc form. It resolves without a registry: anyone can derive the public key from the DID alone. It replaces an opaque hex fingerprint with a standardized W3C DID identifier.

Regulator-grade entity identity

A 20-character alphanumeric code issued by the Global LEI System (GLEIF). Banks, PSPs and supervisors already consume LEIs. It optionally binds an OpenChainGraph vendor, and their signing key, to a legal entity that regulators can look up directly.

One identifier on both layers

The same LEI can appear in publisher.lei (who signed this artifact) and in debtor.lei / creditor.lei (party to this payment). One identifier serves the provenance signing layer and the ISO 20022 financial payload layer.

Identity stays outside the preimage

publisher.lei and the keyid form describe who produced the artifact rather than what decision was made. Neither enters the execution_hash preimage: a verifier written for v0.1 computes identical hashes on v0.3 artifacts carrying full party identity.

Stage 10 of 22 · Advanced · v0.3

The OKF companion bundle carries the knowledge layer

v0.3 publishes an Open Knowledge Format (OKF) companion bundle auto-generated from chaingraph.json: one markdown concept file per Graph Index node. It is the narrative complement to the DCAT catalog. DCAT is what an agent reads to plan a chain before calling anything; OKF is what an agent or a human reads to understand what a tool does and when to use it. OKF concept documents are knowledge and never decision artifacts: they MUST NOT carry an execution_hash.

SPEC §10OKF GuideOKF bundle

Knowledge read before acting

OKF concept documents explain what a tool means, its intent, when to use it, and how its output feeds downstream decisions. An agent reads OKF before choosing tools; a human reads it to understand what is in the catalog. It is the annotation layer rather than the execution map.

The machine-readable execution recipe

The DCAT catalog (chaingraph.json) tells an agent which tools exist, which tools feed which, and which MCP endpoint to call. OKF sits on top of it as the meaning layer, separate by design.

Graph edges become markdown links

The ocg:consumes and ocg:feeds edges in the DCAT catalog become markdown hyperlinks between concept documents, reproducing the graph structure in a human-readable form. One concept file per live node, regenerated on every index change.

Knowledge and decisions stay separate

An OKF concept document MUST NOT carry an execution_hash or an audit_signature. It is knowledge rather than a decision. Nothing in the OpenChainGraph verification path depends on OKF: the bundle is generated, swappable and purely informational.

Stage 11 of 22 · Advanced · v0.3.1

Profile conformance uses dereferenceable URIs

v0.3.1 makes semantic profile conformance machine-resolvable using W3C PROF and Content Negotiation by Profile. A profile is published at a dereferenceable URI and declared with dct:conformsTo, beyond being named by an opaque string token. The semantic_profile token (for example "iso20022:pacs.008-subset") is retained as a registered alias pointing at the URI through a token-URI registry. Every node declares only what it truthfully conforms to: a VaR engine carries no ISO 20022 profile. The hash preimage is unchanged, and all prior artifacts remain valid.

SPEC §11Profile Conformance Guide

A real URL a verifier can fetch

Conformance is declared as dct:conformsTo: ["https://ainumbers.co/chaingraph/profiles/iso20022/pacs008-subset.jsonld"]. The profile URI resolves to a PROF prof:Profile resource listing schema and guidance.

The token stays as shorthand

The semantic_profile string token (for example "iso20022:pacs.008-subset") is retained as a registered alias. The authoritative token-URI map is published at profiles/registry.json. Existing v0.3 artifacts remain valid with no changes.

Declare only real conformance

A payment node gets iso20022:pacs.008-subset. A party-only node gets iso20022:party-identification. A VaR engine or DORA classifier gets no ISO 20022 profile. Forcing a payment profile onto a non-payment node would be a false conformance claim, and this design avoids it.

Compatibility is mathematical

dct:conformsTo, @context and semantic_profile all sit outside the execution_hash preimage (sorted-key SHA-256 over {policy_parameters, output_payload}). Backward compatibility holds by construction.

Stage 12 of 22 · Practitioner · v0.4

Server-side kernels make every tool agent-native

v0.4 introduces Compute Binding: a registered kernel (pure ESM exporting compute(), buildArtifact() and meta) runs directly on the Cloudflare Worker for nodes where gpu:false. An AI agent sends policy_parameters in a single tools/call and receives a complete, fully hashed v0.4 artifact in one round-trip, with no browser delegation, no user interaction and no waiting for a page to load. The new compute_mode: "server" envelope field tells every downstream consumer how the hash was computed, and it is excluded from the hash preimage, so all prior artifacts remain valid.

SPEC §12Integration GuideTry it live: run a workflow

Kernels that run server-side

A v0.4 kernel exports three things: compute(params) runs the calculation server-side; buildArtifact(params, output) assembles the OCG envelope with execution_hash; meta declares the kernel name and tool version. Kernels are registered in kernels/index.mjs and vendored into data/ at build time.

compute_mode in the envelope

The envelope carries compute_mode: "server" when the Worker ran the kernel, excluded from the hash preimage. The compute_capability field in chaingraph.json advertises which nodes support server-side compute, so agents discover it before calling. compute_mode: "auto" lets the Worker decide.

One round-trip instead of a detour

Before v0.4, a tool call returned a delegation URL the agent had to open in a browser, which required a JS runtime and user interaction. v0.4 collapses this to one MCP tools/call: the agent sends parameters and receives a complete signed artifact. The spec calls this agent-native execution.

The privacy boundary holds

§12.4 keeps the Worker kernel in a stateless context. No PII is logged and no computation telemetry is emitted. The boundary matches the MCP contract's readOnlyHint: true: sensitive policy_parameters never leave the call and response envelope.

Stage 13 of 22 · Practitioner · v0.4

The hash travels to the last mile in every export

v0.4 defines the chaingraph_export umbrella: xlsx, pdf, csv and xbrl renderings generated from a verified artifact, with chaingraph_export:vc added in v0.4.1 (the next panel). The invariant: exports are produced downstream of execution_hash and are excluded from the hash preimage. The cryptographic anchor is fixed before any export runs. The export_artifact MCP tool (readOnlyHint: true) takes an artifact and a format and returns a deterministic rendering, and the execution_hash travels intact into every output as embedded metadata. Regulatory XBRL submissions use the xbrl_taxonomy parameter (for example "eba-corep-own-funds") to select the filing profile.

SPEC §13Export Profiles GuideTry it live: run a workflow

The v0.4 export umbrella

Four profiles ship in v0.4: xlsx (a three-sheet analyst workbook: Decision, Summary, Provenance), pdf (signed printable render), csv (tabular data extract) and xbrl (machine-readable regulatory submission). Each is generated from output_payload after the execution_hash is already fixed.

export_artifact

The agent calls export_artifact(artifact, format, xbrl_taxonomy?). The tool generates the rendering on demand and returns it, changing nothing on the server. Nodes advertise it through export_capability in chaingraph.json so agents can discover it before calling.

Exports sit outside the hash

The chaingraph_export field and all export outputs are outside the hash preimage. The hash is computed over {policy_parameters, output_payload} only, as in every prior version. Existing artifacts remain valid with no re-hashing. The export is the last mile rather than the source of truth.

Machine-readable for regulators

Pass xbrl_taxonomy: "eba-corep-own-funds" for a bank capital COREP filing. The XBRL instance document embeds execution_hash as a named fact, so regulators can trace every submitted number back to the verified artifact that produced it, with no spreadsheet chain of custody.

Three layers: the verified JSON artifact (provenance-out), the OKF concept bundle (knowledge-in, panel 15), and the export renderings (rendered-for-review). Only the first layer carries an execution_hash and enters verification chains. The export layer annotates the artifact without modifying it.

Stage 14 of 22 · Advanced · v0.4.1

Proof Binding turns tamper-evidence into authenticated attestation

Stage 17 of 22 · Advanced · v0.2+

How an Ed25519 signing key lives and rotates

Panel 11 covered what the signature does. This panel covers how the key is born, where it lives, how verification finds it and how it gets rotated. The lifecycle is: Generate (crypto.subtle.generateKey in the browser, or Node's crypto.generateKeyPairSync server-side), Publish (export the public key as hex, derive the did:key, add it to chaingraph.json under ocg:signing_keys), Sign (kernels/_proof.mjs or the in-toto envelope signer produces the signature bytes), Verify (any verifier imports the public key from the Graph Index and calls crypto.subtle.verify). Rotation adds a new entry with a valid_from date, and old artifacts signed under the prior key remain permanently verifiable through the prior entry.

SPEC §5

Generate

crypto.subtle.generateKey({name:"Ed25519"}, true, ["sign","verify"]) produces a CryptoKeyPair. Export the public key as raw bytes, encode as hex, then derive the did:key (multicodec 0xed01 prefix plus base58btc). Never export the private key bytes outside a secure context.

Publish

Add the public key to the tool's Graph Index entry: ocg:signing_keys: [{keyid:"did:key:z6Mk…", valid_from:"2025-01-01"}]. Any verifier that fetches chaingraph.json from the tool's endpoint can derive the public key from the did:key and verify anything signed with it.

Sign

For in-toto, sign the base64url-encoded artifact body (the PAE envelope). For Proof Binding, sign the JCS-canonicalized document via kernels/_proof.mjs. Both use crypto.subtle.sign("Ed25519", privateKey, data). The output is 64 bytes, encoded into the artifact.

Rotate without breaking history

Add a new key entry with today's valid_from, and set valid_until on the old entry if desired. Verifiers MUST use the key whose valid_from and valid_until bracket the artifact's created_at timestamp. Rotation is additive, so old artifacts stay verifiable permanently.

Stage 19 of 22 · Advanced · v0.6

Kernel Identity Binding pins which logic ran

Every previous stage showed that execution_hash proves an output follows from its inputs. It says nothing about which code performed the computation. A dishonest server could substitute a different kernel (one with backdoors, different rounding or different regulatory logic) while still producing a valid hash over its outputs. v0.6 closes this gap with Kernel Identity Binding (§17): the artifact MAY record a kernel_digest, a SHA-256 over the LF-normalised kernel source bytes, at audit_signature.build_identity. A verifier performs a three-way cross-check: the artifact kernel_digest equals the Graph Index compute_images[].image_id and equals the digest recomputed from the published source file. Any mismatch fails the binding. The §17.2 caveat states it plainly: this is an advisory claim rather than a cryptographic proof of execution, because a dishonest server could record the right digest while running different code. That is what §18 solves.

SPEC §17

The digest itself

_buildid.mjs reads the kernel file, normalises all line endings to LF, and runs WebCrypto SHA-256 over the UTF-8 bytes. The result, sha256:<hex>, is stored at audit_signature.build_identity.kernel_digest. Deterministic: the same source always gives the same digest regardless of OS or runtime.

The public claim in the index

Each live node in chaingraph.json SHOULD publish its kernel digest in compute_images[]: compute_images: [{system:"sha256-source", image_id:"sha256:…", valid_from:"…"}]. This is the second leg of the three-way cross-check, the public and independently verifiable record of which kernel a node claims to run.

Three-way cross-check

The verifier compares (1) artifact.build_identity.kernel_digest, (2) chaingraph.json compute_images[].image_id, and (3) the digest recomputed from the published source file. All three must match. A tampered artifact, a stale Graph Index or a modified source fails the binding immediately.

Where the claim stops

§17 cannot prevent a dishonest server from recording the correct digest while running different code. It strengthens tamper-evidence (the digest is inside the §16 secured set when both apply) and makes forgery auditable, but it does not prove the named kernel produced the output. §18 exists for that.

Stage 20 of 22 · Advanced · v0.6

A Compute-Integrity Proof shows correct execution succinctly

§17 pins which kernel was claimed; §18 makes the claim cryptographic. An artifact MAY attach an optional zkVM receipt at audit_signature.compute_proof: a zero-knowledge proof that this specific program (identified by imageId) produced this specific output (recorded in journal) from some inputs, without the verifier re-running the kernel and optionally without revealing those inputs at all. This is OCG's analogue of Trusted Compute Units (arXiv:2504.15717), and it is software-only: no TEE, no hardware enclave, no blockchain anchor. The system is agnostic (risc0, sp1, jolt); groth16-bn254 (256 bytes, uncompressed BN254 A, B, C) is recommended. STARK seal verification is delegated to the named system's vetted verifier, exactly as §4 delegates SHA-256 to WebCrypto and §16 delegates Ed25519; for the recommended Groth16 format OCG ships its own self-contained BN254 pairing check (§18.1). Proving is off-band: it requires a Rust toolchain and heavy compute, and it MUST NOT be claimed to run in the browser or the Worker. Default-off.

The receipt object

{type:"ZkVmReceipt", system:"risc0"|"sp1"|"jolt", receiptFormat:"groth16-bn254"|"stark", imageId:"sha256:…", seal:"<base64>", journal:{output:…}}. It lives at audit_signature.compute_proof, hash-excluded, under the tolerant audit_signature object. A v0.6 artifact still validates under the frozen v0.4 schema.

Program identity binding

imageId in the receipt MUST match compute_images[].image_id in the Graph Index node entry, the published claim of which program the node runs. Together with the §17 kernel_digest this gives a two-layer program identity check: source digest (advisory) plus zkVM guest identity (cryptographic).

Output binding

The receipt's journal.output MUST equal the artifact's output_payload. The verifier checks this binding directly, with no re-execution. If the journal matches and the seal verifies, the verifier holds cryptographic assurance that this program produced this output.

Confidential inputs (§18.3)

When inputs are confidential, policy_parameters carries commitments or hashes instead of cleartext. §4 recompute then becomes unavailable to third parties and the receipt is the sole verification path. A tool in this mode MUST surface that to users. Like §16, default-off.

The verifiable-computation ladder (§18.4)

L1 is the §4 execution_hash: tamper-evidence, with the verifier re-running under cleartext inputs. L2 is the §16 Proof Binding: authenticated attestation, where a named key vouches for the whole artifact. L3 is the §18 Compute-Integrity Proof: a succinct proof of correct execution, checked from a receipt with no re-run and optionally no revealed inputs. They compose: a §18 receipt MAY itself be covered by a §16 proof. Proving stays off-band and MUST NOT be claimed to run in the browser or the Worker.

Stage 21 of 22 · Practitioner · §15

How the standard polices itself

A rulebook nobody checks is a wish list. Most specifications are prose: they say a conformant tool should do this and must not do that, and everyone hopes implementers read carefully. OpenChainGraph takes a stricter stance, and it is the reason the earlier panels can be trusted.

Every sentence in the standard that says MUST has to name a script that fails the build the moment the rule is broken. Conformance is a test that either passes or does not. A rule with no gate is outside the standard: if nothing can fail when the rule is broken, the rule is decoration.

SPEC §15The receipt-conformance corpus, explainedTry it live: run a workflow

Every MUST is executable

Each normative rule points at a real check. "The hash preimage is built exactly one way" is a gate that re-derives the hash and fails if a tool used any other recipe.

A gate that watches the gates

One check reads the rule-to-gate matrix and fails the build if any numbered rule has no gate attached. Nobody can quietly add a rule and forget to enforce it, and nobody can delete a gate and leave the rule stranded.

Prose alone does not count

If a proposed rule cannot be expressed as something a machine can check, it does not enter the standard as a MUST. It stays advice. That keeps the normative core small, precise and resistant to fakery.

What a green build means

You do not audit a vendor's good intentions. The standard ships with its own enforcement, so a green build obeyed every rule, and a build that broke a rule never reached anyone.

Three of the gates a build passes before anything ships

preimage. One canonical way to build the hash input. The gate re-computes every published hash and rejects any tool that shortcut the recipe.

coverage. The meta-gate: every numbered rule in the standard maps to a named check. A rule with no check fails the whole build.

page parity. The friendly spec page you read cannot drift from the written standard. If the page shows a section the standard does not define, the build stops.

Stage 22 of 22 · Practitioner · §20

Anchoring artifacts in time

An artifact's execution_hash proves what a tool computed. It does not prove when. Section 20 closes that gap by defining the anchor_bindings array, which sits outside the hash scope so an artifact's integrity proof is unchanged while it carries one or more independent timestamp receipts. Each binding names a timestamp authority, carries the hash that was sent, and embeds the signed response. The authority never sees your file, only the hex digest.

SPEC §20Try it live: anchor a document

You send 32 bytes and receive a signed RFC 3161 receipt with no account required

Integrity is preserved

anchor_bindings sits outside the execution_hash preimage. Adding it after the fact does not change the hash: §4 verification passes on the same artifact before and after anchoring. The two checks are fully independent.

Proof of existence in time

The authority's signed timestamp proves the hash existed before the receipt was issued. Combined with the artifact's execution_hash you hold proof of both the computation's content and the moment it occurred.

No server required to verify

A DER receipt verifies with openssl ts -verify and the issuing CA's root certificate. No network call, no account, no expiry. The receipt is self-contained evidence that survives any server going offline.

One receipt covers a chain

Anchor the terminal node's artifact and the full provenance graph is covered. Every prior node's hash sits in parent_hashes, so the timestamp receipt anchors the whole chain by transitivity.

Supported binding types in §20

rfc3161-tst. Certificate-backed timestamp. DigiCert, Sectigo, Sigstore TSA, FreeTSA or GitHub TSA. DER-encoded TimeStampToken, confirmed immediately, verifiable with openssl ts -verify and the issuing CA's root certificate, which the standard pins.

opentimestamps. Bitcoin-anchored proof. The hash is batched into a Bitcoin transaction by the OTS calendar network, with no fee and no wallet. The proof is pending for a few hours after stamping; once confirmed the block timestamp is the permanent evidence.

To anchor an OCG artifact, drop it on the Anchor page at anchor.ainumbers.co. The page reads the execution_hash field directly, with no re-hashing of the raw file, and emits the artifact with anchor_bindings appended per §20. The anchor_document_integrity MCP tool exposes the same operation for use inside multi-step workflows.

28
Frequently asked · Beginner

Questions we keep hearing

These come up every time someone meets OpenChainGraph for the first time, covering the plain-language basics, the core standard, the v0.4 additions, v0.5 Proof Binding and the AIN Bridge integration layer.

Is this a blockchain?

No. OpenChainGraph has no coin and no network to join. There is also no shared ledger that strangers must agree on: each tool runs on its own and stamps its result with a fingerprint, and you trust that result by redoing the math yourself.

The OpenChainGraph and Blockchains page shows where the two designs overlap and where they differ, in three short animations. For the technical comparison with blockchains, Git and supply-chain attestations, see "Isn't this basically a blockchain?" further down.

What does "chain" mean here?

A chain is the named, ordered list of steps defined in chaingraph.json. SPEC §21 calls it a chains[] entry: {name, steps: [{tool_id, ...}]}. That is the noun. In plain language people also call a chain a workflow; when this page says run a workflow it means run a chain.

Running a chain is what produces the fingerprint trail: each step copies the prior step's execution_hash into its own parent_hashes, so decisions link into a tamper-evident line you can trace end to end. That trail is what a chain produces when it runs, beyond what a chain is.

What is a node?

A node is a tool registered in the Graph Index, viewed as one vertex of the citation graph. It exists once registered, whether or not any chain's step list places it. Being placed in a chain's step list is one way a node picks up citation edges rather than what makes it a node (SPEC §0.1).

Some tools on this site, the DLT and permissioned-network topology tools under Guides, also use "node" for a network participant. That is a different concept from the one on this page, so do not carry the meaning across.

Is a tool the same as a node?

Close, with a distinction. A tool is the callable unit itself, either a browser page or the identical computation exposed as an MCP-callable tool (tool_id in SPEC §21). A node is that tool viewed as a vertex in the citation graph once it is registered in the Graph Index; chain placement is one way it picks up edges rather than a requirement for being a node.

Ask for a tool by name and you get one page or one MCP call. Ask about a node and you are asking about its place in the citation graph, which may or may not include a chain's step list.

Who is this for?

Three audiences read the same page from different doors. A compliance officer or auditor gets a decision trail that proves what ran and that nothing was altered after the fact, without trusting a vendor's word. An engineer gets a small, precise standard with real gates and one canonical way to build a hash. An AI agent gets tools it can discover, call and verify on its own over MCP.

The Beginner, Practitioner and Advanced label on each panel marks the natural entry point for each reader, so you can skim to your level and go deeper only where you need to.

Isn't this basically a blockchain? You're literally chaining hashes together.

The similarity is real: both designs use hash pointers to link records into a graph. A blockchain adds distributed consensus, a network of nodes that must agree on one shared, append-only ledger. OpenChainGraph has no such network. Each vendor runs its own tool and publishes its own artifact, and nothing needs mining or validating.

The closer analogy is Git's object model, a directed acyclic graph of content-addressed objects where you trust a record by recomputing its hash yourself. Another is in-toto and SLSA supply-chain attestations, which hash an artifact and sign it so anyone can verify it independently. OpenChainGraph applies that pattern to financial decision artifacts instead of software build artifacts. The OpenChainGraph and Blockchains page animates the comparison and shows where the two meet, such as timestamps anchored in Bitcoin.

What happens to old artifact hashes if a tool is updated or the model changes?

Old hashes remain permanently valid for the inputs and outputs they were computed over; the hash does not expire. If a tool changes its computation logic (say, a risk model update) and you re-run the same policy_parameters, the new output_payload will differ, producing a new and different execution_hash. That is correct behavior: it is a different decision.

The recommended practice is to version your tools explicitly: tool_version: "1.2.0" inside the artifact envelope. A downstream tool that cites an artifact from v1.1.0 is permanently linked to that exact version's output. Auditors can distinguish "the credit model on 14 March 2026" from "the updated model on 1 April 2026" by comparing the parent hashes, which makes model changes auditable by design.

Can I use OpenChainGraph without MCP? We're not running an AI agent stack yet.

Yes. MCP is an optional integration layer. The core of OpenChainGraph is a JSON envelope and a SHA-256 hash function. Any tool that emits a JSON artifact with execution_hash and chain.parent_hashes is conformant, regardless of transport: a REST API, a message queue, a file drop, an email attachment or a direct database write all work.

MCP becomes relevant for cross-vendor chaining (panel 8) when you want agents to autonomously discover, call and chain tools from a chaingraph.json Graph Index. Before that, the value is simpler: tamper-evident audit trails across your existing pipeline, with no new infrastructure. Add MCP when you're ready; the artifacts are already compatible.

How does this relate to the agentic payment standards such as AP2 and x402?

Those standards govern payment execution: the mechanics of how an AI agent authorizes and settles a transaction. OpenChainGraph governs decision provenance: the verifiable record of why a decision (including a payment decision) was made, what inputs drove it, and which prior decisions it depended on.

They are complementary layers of the same stack. An AP2-conformant agentic mandate might cite an OpenChainGraph artifact as its authorization evidence: "This payment was authorized because the liquidity stress tool (hash: a3f9…) determined headroom was sufficient, which itself cited the FX hedge optimizer (hash: 7c2b…)." The payment rails handle settlement; OpenChainGraph handles the audit trail upstream of it. Neither competes with the other.

How stable is the standard? Can I implement against it now?

The SPEC carries the status "NORMATIVE, Single Source of Truth" and is at version 0.8.13, with the hash construction, the artifact envelope and the verification procedure stable and implementable today: the reference implementation exists and runs under gates. What matures on a slower clock is the ecosystem: most MCP tool catalogs are still young, most vendors do not yet emit structured JSON artifacts at all, and cross-vendor chaining needs two parties who both follow the spec.

The recommended posture: implement the artifact envelope now, in any tool that produces decisions your team or your regulators will need to audit later. The lift is small, one SHA-256 call and a JSON wrapper. When the ecosystem catches up (agentic payment stacks going mainstream, MCP tool catalogs proliferating, regulators asking for machine-verifiable decision trails), you will already be producing conformant artifacts. Standards that arrive late to adoption cycles rarely win.

What's the difference between a Policy Mandate (.policy.json) and an OCG artifact? Both seem to carry an execution_hash.

They serve different layers. An OCG artifact is the canonical provenance record, the tamper-evident envelope whose execution_hash is the SHA-256 of {policy_parameters, output_payload}. It is designed for auditors, regulators and downstream tools that need to verify a decision independently. The artifact is the source of truth.

A Policy Mandate (.policy.json) is a browser-side handoff format. AIN Bridge produces one when a tool finishes, packaging the tool's inputs and key outputs into a file the user can drop into the next tool's intake zone. The execution_hash field in the mandate is inherited from the artifact for traceability; the mandate itself is not hashed. Mandates are ephemeral transit containers and artifacts are the permanent record. If they conflict, the artifact wins.

If Excel and PDF exports are excluded from the execution_hash preimage, how do I know the exported file wasn't tampered with after it left the server?

The export does not carry a hash of itself, but it embeds the original execution_hash as metadata, and that is enough to anchor it. If someone alters the numbers in the Excel file, the execution_hash in the Provenance sheet no longer matches what you get when you re-run the tool with the recorded policy_parameters. The mismatch is the tamper signal.

For high-stakes auditing, go back to the canonical JSON artifact (beyond the xlsx), re-run verify_execution_hash, and confirm the output_payload matches what the exported file shows. The export is a rendering for humans; the artifact is what is actually tamper-evident. This is the three-layer model: provenance-out (canonical JSON), knowledge-in (OKF), rendered-for-review (exports). Only the first layer is in the verification chain.

Does compute_mode:"server" change how I verify the hash? Do I need access to the original server or kernel to reproduce it?

No: compute_mode is completely outside the hash preimage. The hash computation has not changed since v0.1: SHA-256 over the RFC 8785 canonical serialization of {policy_parameters, output_payload} with keys sorted. Whether a server ran the kernel, a browser ran the tool, or someone computed the artifact by hand, the math is the same.

What compute_mode: "server" tells you is how the tool was executed, beyond what was hashed. A verifier that ignores the field entirely still computes an identical hash. Every v0.4 field outside the preimage is strictly informational: it annotates the artifact without affecting its verifiability. A v0.1 verifier from 2025 produces the same result on a v0.4 artifact from 2026.

Is OpenChainGraph only for financial decisions? We're building in healthcare, logistics, or legal.

No. The core spec is domain-agnostic. The mandate_type field is a free string: use "diagnostic_result", "routing_decision", "eligibility_check" or anything else. The hash construction, the chain model, the MCP integration layer and Compute Binding work identically regardless of domain.

The ISO 20022 semantic profile (panel 13) and the LEI / did:key identity model (panel 14) are optional addenda for payment tool vendors; they do not affect the core hash preimage and are not required for conformance. The fintech examples in this explainer reflect where the spec authors work rather than a constraint on who can implement it. If your tool takes structured inputs, runs a deterministic computation and produces outputs someone might later need to audit or dispute, OpenChainGraph applies.

Can I adopt Compute Binding (§12) without Export Profiles (§13), or vice versa? Do I need both for v0.4 conformance?

They are fully independent. §12 addresses where computation runs (server or browser); §13 addresses how verified results are rendered for human consumption. You can ship a gpu:false server-side kernel without ever implementing export_artifact, and you can implement the xlsx, pdf and xbrl export profiles for browser-computed tools without touching Compute Binding.

Capability discovery in chaingraph.json is per-node: compute_capability: "server" and export_capability: ["xlsx","pdf"] are separate fields. An agent calling list_ainumbers_tools sees exactly which capabilities each node advertises and routes accordingly. The chaingraph_version: "v0.4" field in the envelope signals that the emitting tool understands the v0.4 schema; it does not imply both features are implemented.

Does Proof Binding make OpenChainGraph artifacts PII-laden?

No. Proof Binding is default-off, and inputs (policy_parameters) remain PII-free under the standard zero-egress contract. What changes is that the signer's key identity becomes linkable to the run. The artifact itself carries no personal data. Tools that offer signing MUST clearly communicate that opting in de-anonymizes at the key level rather than the payload level.

The zero_pii_verified semantics are unchanged. If you sign with a did:key generated fresh for each session and never published, the run remains practically unlinkable. If you sign with an institutional did:web anchored to your domain, the signer becomes publicly known, which is exactly the point for regulated institutions seeking auditor-grade attestation.

What's the difference between audit_signature.signatures[] (v0.2 / §4) and audit_signature.proof (v0.5 / §16)?

signatures[] (§4, v0.2) is a DSSE/in-toto envelope: it signs over a payload field that is a base64url encoding of the artifact without audit_signature. It is the v0.2 signing mechanism, optimized for in-toto and Sigstore toolchain compatibility.

proof (§16, v0.5) is a W3C Data Integrity proof using the eddsa-jcs-2022 cryptosuite. It signs over the whole artifact minus proof itself, using the same JCS canonicalizer as the §4 hash construction, so no second canonicalization path exists. The two mechanisms co-exist in the same audit_signature object, are independent, and serve different verifier ecosystems: in-toto and Sigstore consumers use signatures[]; W3C VC and Data Integrity verifiers use proof. Both are optional, and a fully signed v0.5 artifact carries both.
