Inverted Database Forgeries Explainer

Four ways to forge a row in the inverted database, and the layer that catches each one. Every hash on this page is a real value from the published estate, and your tab recomputes it while you read.

Presenter mode shows one step per screen. Arrow keys move, A toggles autoplay, Esc exits.
1
Step 1

From demo hashes to the estate’s own

The original inverted-database page computed real SHA-256 digests, but over demo values it chose itself, and its footer says so: those hashes differ from the kernels’ canonical execution hash. This page closes that gap. Every hash here is one the estate itself published, and your tab re-derives each one from the published inputs before you finish reading.

Nothing about the inversion changes. What changes is the evidence: the bytes hashed here are the canonical bytes the kernels, the registry and the log already commit to.

2
Step 2

Two lanes of real published nodes

Lane one is a chain: a loan payment schedule feeds a disclosure checklist, and an audit-trail aggregator folds the results into a session receipt root. Lane two is the AP2 mandate validator on its own. The inputs are each kernel’s published fixture vector, synthetic by construction. The hashes are the canonical ones the estate computed when it published these nodes.

The gold value on the right is the session receipt root: the join operation of the table below, computed over the three execution hashes. Your tab recomputes all four values, and the check table later on this page shows the comparison.

3
Step 3

The same four operations and their proofs

The original page contrasted a usual database with this inverted one. This page adds the third column the original left as a promise: for each of the four operations, the concrete published artifact that proves it happened and has not changed.

Insert is proven by the canonical execution hash. Lookup by presence in the registry tree. Join by the session receipt root. Commit by inclusion in the public log. Each of the next four steps forges one of these and shows the layer that catches it.

4
Step 4

One edited digit and its new hash

The first forgery does not break anything: it takes a real kernel, a real fixture vector, changes one digit of one input field, and reruns the computation correctly. The result is a well-formed receipt for a computation nobody asked for. Nothing errors. The only evidence is the hash.

The forged rerun's hash appears nowhere in the ledger: it matches neither the fixture's golden hash nor any published value. This is the layer the kernels themselves defend.

5
Step 5

Swap the code, not the inputs

The second forgery edits the kernel source itself. A kernel’s registry digest is the SHA-256 of its source file, so a one-line edit produces a different digest, and that digest was never published. The registry can prove the absence, not just fail to find it.

The forged digest sorts strictly between two neighbouring published keys, and each neighbour carries an audit path to the committed root: a real ics23 non-existence proof under ainumbers-simple-v1, generated by the estate's own builder. For the real digest the same builder refuses: absence of a present key is not constructible. The proof of a negative in depth is the companion page linked in the sources.

6
Step 6

Quietly edit the archive

The third forgery leaves the computation alone and edits the stored receipt afterwards: one character inside the journal a bundle publishes, with the digest label left as it was. A reader who trusts the label never notices.

The bundle’s digest exists precisely so the label cannot be trusted: the journal’s canonical bytes hash to the label, and one changed character moves the hash. Your tab recomputes both.

7
Step 7

Rewrite the past

The fourth forgery replaces the recorded history: a rewritten tree head that claims the estate always said what the forger wants. Against a plain log this is undetectable, because there is nothing to compare the rewrite with.

The bundle's inclusion proof is the comparison. The path of 5 sibling hashes, walked bottom-up from the bundle's leaf at index 77056, rebuilds exactly one root: the signed one. It cannot be made to reach any other head, and the signed head is anchored in a transparency log outside this estate.

8
Step 8

The commit layer above all four

All three receipt bundles cite the same anchored hash: the registry lineage checkpoint. One entry in the public log, cosigned by thirteen witnesses under a two-of-three quorum policy, commits every published bundle at once. Forging one bundle means fighting that anchor; forging all of them means rewriting a public log that the estate does not control.

This is why the four layers stack. A forger must defeat the execution hash, the registry tree, the journal digest and the public anchor, and each layer’s evidence is checkable without trusting the estate’s word for any of it.
