Side by side with VCP

The VeritasChain Protocol records what an algorithmic trading system decided and executed, in an append-only, hash-chained, externally anchored log. Our receipts record what a computation did, in a deterministic, browser-verifiable artifact chain. This page reads the two formats against the same questions: what does a record bind, how is completeness proven, who can verify it without trusting the producer, and where does each format stop. It was written for a live correspondence with a practitioner on the VCP side, and for anyone comparing record formats for audit or regulatory work.

Everything about VCP here is read from their published specification v1.2, marked Production Ready, dated 6 July 2026, retrieved 15 October 2026 at commit b23acb266f88 of github.com/veritaschain/vcp-spec. Everything about our side is live on this site today. No connector, bridge, or implementation exists between the two formats, and nothing on this page claims otherwise. Part three pins both sides to exact revisions and classifies every field of one artifact per side into three classes: hash-preimage, metadata, and externally trusted. Part four turns the correspondence's 25 October review into the page's review basis: a per-row evidence table, a fourth check for declared-set completeness, and a negative battery with verdicts written down in advance.

Part one · The two record formats

What each format binds and how

VCP separates integrity into three named layers. Our receipts bind one canonical hash per run and hang verification off determinism. The table in step 3 puts the two side by side row by row.

1
Step 1 · The other format

What VCP asks a producer to keep

VCP is published by the VeritasChain Standards Organization in Tokyo as the financial-domain profile of their Verifiable AI Provenance framework. Version 1.2 reached GA on 6 July 2026. A producer records its trading events in an append-only log; the spec's own scope line says the log verifies integrity and attribution of recorded events and does not certify the correctness or safety of the underlying AI or trading decisions. That boundary is written by VSO themselves, and it rhymes with the boundary our own artifacts declare.

Anchoring is where VCP is strictest: every tier must anchor externally, on a clock budget that tightens from Silver's 24 hours to Platinum's 10 minutes. Acceptable targets include Bitcoin, OpenTimestamps, and RFC 3161 timestamp authorities. The spec sets expectations per tier plainly: Silver, in VSO's own words on their site, provides transparency rather than regulatory-grade evidence.

Step 2 · The home format

What our receipts keep

An OpenChainGraph receipt binds one computation. Its execution hash is a SHA-256 over the canonical JSON of exactly the declared inputs and the output payload, produced through the shared hash library; the kernel that computed the output is pinned by its own digest. Workflows chain receipts by carrying each parent hash forward, and the public ledger stores the artifacts content-addressed so the hash is the address.

The full anatomy, including a synthetic run you can reproduce from its published witness, is the subject of our companion page Four hashes and a witness. The short version for this crosswalk: our format proves a computation by reconstruction rather than by attestation, keeps private figures out of the record as salted commitments, and publishes receipts to a ledger any browser can verify without a server.

Part two · Differences, common ground, and the unbuilt bridges

Beyond the table

Three differences survive the table because they are philosophy rather than mechanism. After them: the primitives both formats already share, and the connectors that do not exist yet, described without pretending otherwise.

4
Step 4 · Philosophy

Three differences that matter

Most of the table is mechanism, where either format could adopt the other's answer. Three rows are philosophy, and those are the ones a standards discussion should settle first.

The erasure row deserves one extra sentence. VCP's annex is candid that destroying the decryption key satisfies an erasure obligation only in some jurisdictions, and that it sits badly with reproducibility-style retention rules such as SEC Rule 17a-4. Our format's answer, holding private figures outside the record from the first byte, sidesteps that tension and pays for it elsewhere: a commitment without the witness is a claim nobody can bind to real figures. Which answer fits depends on what the auditor is allowed to ask for.

Prompt at this stepcompare the GDPR answers

VCP shreds what it stored (the record stays, the plaintext becomes unrecoverable); OCG never stored the figures (the record holds only a salted commitment). VCP keeps full audit richness and accepts non-recoverability; OCG keeps verifiability and accepts that only the witness-holder can bind commitment to figure.

Step 5 · Common ground

The primitives both formats already share

A crosswalk is easier when the floors are the same. Both formats default to Ed25519, hash with SHA-256, build Merkle structures for completeness, cite the IETF SCITT architecture, and treat external anchoring as the escape hatch from trusting the producer. Those shared floors are why a field-level mapping is possible at all.

The SCITT row is the one to watch. VCP v1.2 defines normative opt-in alignment fields targeting COSE Receipts, and the profile Internet-Draft for VCP on SCITT is an individual submission by Kamimura, the practitioner this correspondence is with. Our receipts were built with the same mapping in mind. Neither side has implemented the handshake; the vocabulary for it exists on paper on both sides of the table.

Part three · Revisions, preimage classes, and the first test

The pinned map

A mapping becomes checkable when both sides are pinned to exact revisions and every field is placed in one of three classes: inside the hash preimage, recorded metadata outside it, or a claim that needs an external trust source. This part does that for one artifact per side, then proposes the first joint test.

Step 8 · The pins

The pinned revisions

Nothing below works against a moving target, so both sides are pinned to exact revisions before any field is classified. The OCG side pins its specification, its specimen run, and the kernel digests that produced it. The VCP side pins the v1.2 specification file by commit, the framework draft it profiles, and the SCITT profile Internet-Draft the correspondence keeps circling.

The four-check line at the bottom is the rule the rest of this part follows. C1 asks whether a recorded hash matches its recorded preimage. C2 asks whether re-running the pinned producer reproduces it. C3 asks whether the declared inputs match the world, and both formats decline to answer that in writing. C4, added per the recipient's 25 October review, asks whether the captured record covers a declared expected set fixed from a source independent of the record itself (step 13). Every field in steps 9 and 10 sits in exactly one of three classes: preimage (C1 reads it), metadata (C1 ignores it), or external trust (no check on this page covers it).

Step 9 · Three classes

The preimage map

The two formats hash different bytes. VCP concatenates the canonicalized header and payload and hashes the string, with the algorithm selectable per deployment. OCG hashes a single canonicalized two-member object, with SHA-256 fixed. Both cite RFC 8785 for canonicalization, so the difference is structural, not accidental: the same two JSON fragments produce different hashes under the two rules, and neither hash verifies the other's bytes.

Read the two tables together and the asymmetry is structural. VCP puts the clock, the producer identity, and the anchor inside its trust perimeter by design, because a trading record must say when and who under regulatory scrutiny. OCG puts the computation inside its preimage and leaves time, identity, and anchoring as declared metadata, because a computation receipt must stay reproducible without any of them. A field is never trusted in one format because its twin is trusted in the other.

Step 10 · Names are not guarantees

Where similar names diverge

The two vocabularies overlap enough to invite false equivalences: both formats speak of event hashes, Merkle trees, signatures, anchors, timestamps, and commitments. Each row below takes one shared word and states why the two guarantees behind it differ. This is the table to read before treating any single field as interchangeable.

Shared wordWhy the guarantees differ
EventHash ↔ execution_hashVCP concatenates two canonicalized parts with a selectable algorithm and includes the header; OCG hashes one two-member JCS object with SHA-256 fixed and excludes identity fields. Same family, different preimages.
Merkle completenessVCP proves batch inclusion at anchor time via RFC 6962 trees. OCG proves exact computation by deterministic reconstruction over chained receipts. Neither mechanism covers the other's blind spot.
signatureVCP: the producer signs every Merkle root, REQUIRED. OCG: the envelope ships empty on standard runs; §27.12 countersigning is scoped to two-party runs.
anchorVCP: external authority on a tier clock, REQUIRED. OCG: no authority attached to standard runs; the Sigsum lineage checkpoint is published beside the estate, not per run.
timestampVCP: dual-format with tier-synced clock SLAs down to PTPv2. OCG: a single operator-asserted field outside the preimage.
commitmentVCP ERASURE: destroy the key over stored plaintext. OCG §25: salted hash over figures that were never stored. Opposite answers to the same GDPR pressure.

The practical rule the correspondence settled on: read the two tables together only at the concern level, never at the field-name level. A shared word is an invitation to compare the underlying guarantees, and the comparison usually ends in a deliberate difference rather than a gap.

Step 11 · The proposal

The first side-by-side test

The correspondence settled on a concrete opening move: one artifact each, three-class tables filled from each side's own pinned text, and the replay check executed rather than assumed. Five steps, all doable on paper and in a terminal, none requiring the other side's permission.

  1. Each side pins one artifact: our CCP specimen from the companion page, and a Platinum-tier sample event from the nasdaq-rta conformance dataset.
  2. Each side fills the three-class table for its own artifact from its own pinned text, without borrowing the other's field names.
  3. C1 and C2 are kept separate per the recipient's 25 October review: we recompute the specimen's hash from its own recorded bytes (C1, offline), then replay it from the published witness through the pinned kernel (C2); the VCP event hash is recomputed from its header and payload per §6.1.1 (C1; the sample carries no producer process to replay).
  4. The two tables are read together at the concern level only, with any shared word checked against the non-equivalence table from step 10.
  5. C3 is written down as out of scope for both, in each side's own words, so the boundary travels with the mapping.

Steps 1 and 2 are complete on our side: the tables in step 9 are that classification, filled from the pinned texts and reproducible from this page. Steps 3 through 5 are joint by nature. Dated 25 October: C1 and C2 are executed and named separately; the VCP half is C1-only until producer-process disclosure exists. If the test surfaces a row where the two formats genuinely can be bridged, that row becomes the first connector worth building, and the three sketches in step 6 stop being sketches.

Step 13 · C4

The fourth check

A Merkle inclusion proof places a provided leaf in the anchored tree; it says nothing about whether every expected event was captured. C4, declared-set completeness, fixes the expected set from a source independent of the captured record, then compares. Neither C1, nor C2, nor a Merkle inclusion proof implies it.
